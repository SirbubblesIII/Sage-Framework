## II. SRP/CCC WORKING DEFINITIONS — As Practiced in This Project

These definitions capture how the SRP-CCC framework has actually been used across conversations, not just the theoretical skill file. They represent the "living" version of the methodology as applied to real work.

### SRP: Structure-Reasoning-Presentation

**STRUCTURE (Phase 1) — Define Before You Reason**

Working definition: *The discipline of restating the problem in your own words, mapping what's in play, drawing boundaries, surfacing assumptions (especially fragile ones), and defining concrete success/failure criteria BEFORE generating solutions.*

How it was practiced in this project:
- Goal restated as "what are the genuinely distinct paths this project could take, what does each require, and what would tell you which path is right?" — not just "review my business plan"
- Six key entities mapped with explicit relationships (founder ↔ collaborator need, market ↔ property, scope ↔ complexity, mission ↔ financial structure, capital requirement ↔ financing)
- Boundaries set explicitly: in scope = financial viability, operational complexity, founder sustainability; out of scope = construction details, vendor selection, legal entity comparison
- Five fragile assumptions identified with impact ratings: foot traffic conversion (highest impact), everything-under-one-roof synergy, membership conversion rate, staffing costs at fair wages, property availability
- Success defined concretely: financially self-sustaining, 5,000+ monthly visitors, 200+ members, fair wages, no 80-hour weeks
- Failure defined concretely: cash depletion in months 6–12, burnout repeat, underwhelming traffic from lack of community awareness

Key learning: Structure is the phase most likely to be skipped and most valuable when done. The earlier conversations (Chat 1: Feb 4, 22:10; Chat 2: Feb 4, 23:22) suffered from jumping straight to reasoning without proper structuring — resulting in Claude miscalculating the loan, underweighting Weston's research, and reasoning about a different problem than the one Weston was asking about.

Structure methods used: SWOT-informed mapping with stakeholder analysis

**REASONING (Phase 2) — Think With Genuine Alternatives**

Working definition: *Generating at least two genuinely distinct approaches (different in mechanism/core assumption, not cosmetic variants), seeking evidence both for and against each, naming concrete kill-steps, and designing discriminating tests that would tell you which approach actually holds up.*

How it was practiced in this project:
- Three approaches generated that differ in core assumption:
  - A (Full-Scale Commercial): "The research already proves it — execute at scale"
  - B (Minimum Viable): "Paper proof ≠ operational proof — test small first"
  - C (Cooperative/Nonprofit): "The commercial structure conflicts with the community mission — match structure to values"
- Genuineness test applied: Someone who fully believes in A would find C uncomfortable (gives up control), and vice versa (too slow). This confirms they're genuinely distinct, not cosmetic.
- Evidence sought in BOTH directions for each approach — not just "why A is good" but "what would break A"
- Kill-steps named for each:
  - A: No co-founder found within 6 months → stop (the solo problem isn't solved)
  - B: Fewer than 400 visitors/month after 6 months → core value proposition isn't landing
  - C: Fewer than 30 committed founding members after 3 months → cooperative lacks foundation
- Discriminating tests designed:
  - A: Can you find one committed partner before signing a loan?
  - B: Can a small space break even within 4 months?
  - C: Can 50+ people sign letters of interest in 60 days?
- Source trust levels assigned: Esri data (moderate-strong), primary foot traffic research (moderate-strong), IBISWorld reports (moderate), cashflow template (moderate), industry patterns (inference)
- Epistemic status labels used: fact, inference, assumption, guess, value judgment

Key learning: The earlier conversations demonstrated what happens when reasoning lacks genuine alternatives — Claude initially offered "scale back" as the only real option, which wasn't reasoning but rather a pre-formed conclusion looking for justification. When Weston pushed back, the reasoning had to be rebuilt. This is the "rationalizing vs. reasoning" failure the SRP skill warns about.

Reasoning methods used: Scenario planning with pre-mortem elements

**PRESENTATION (Phase 3) — Make It Usable**

Working definition: *Taking the reasoning and packaging it in a form the person can actually use — leading with the answer, calibrating confidence with numbers tied to evidence quality, making next steps concrete enough to act on, preserving genuine dissent rather than forcing false consensus, and sourcing every claim.*

How it was practiced in this project:
- Answer led upfront: "I recommend a sequenced hybrid of B → C or B → A"
- Confidence calibrated numerically with reasoning: 70% on recommendation (moderate because key assumptions need local verification), 80% on market demand, 85% on pricing, 95% on solo failure risk, 35% on cooperative viability
- Next steps made concrete and ordered: (1) verify property landscape weeks 1-2, (2) run "20 people" test weeks 2-6, (3) launch pop-up months 2-4, (4) decide scaling path months 4-6, (5) update business plan months 6-8
- Dissent preserved: "Maybe A is right and you just need one person," "Maybe C is the project itself," "Maybe the right move is to extract learning and apply elsewhere"
- Kill-steps surfaced as caveats, not buried
- Reusable work from original project explicitly cataloged (not just assessed and discarded)

Presentation methods used: Comparative analysis with preserved dissent

### CCC: Compare-Contrast-Consensus

Working definition: *When multiple approaches exist, synthesize them by mapping where they agree (Compare), where they diverge and what drives the divergence (Contrast), and what can be concluded — with or without full resolution (Consensus). The goal is NOT to force a winner but to make the choice landscape clear.*

How it was practiced in this project:

**COMPARE (Agreements):**
- All three approaches agree: market demand is real, founder isolation must be structurally solved, full activity bundle is too much for day one, downtown Lawrence is the right location
- Convergence from different structural models (commercial, lean startup, cooperative) strengthens confidence in these shared conclusions

**CONTRAST (Divergences):**
- Mapped across 10 dimensions: capital needed, debt burden, time to open, founder risk, control, mission alignment, scalability, what it proves, key risk, what it requires
- Root driver of differences identified: "Where you place the burden of proof" — A says data already proves it, B says prove it operationally, C says prove it collectively
- This is a values difference, not an empirical one — recognizing this prevents false resolution

**CONSENSUS (Synthesis Without Forcing):**
- High-confidence conclusions stated with evidence (market viable, pricing competitive, solo will fail)
- Conditional conclusions preserved: "A is best IF X, B is best IF Y, C is best IF Z"
- Unresolved questions flagged: property availability, collaborator finding capacity, Weston's current relationship to the project
- The recommendation (B → A/C) is explicitly conditional on assumptions that need local verification

## III. CONCEPTS & IDEAS CATALOG — Throughout the Project

### A. Business Concepts

1. **The Third Place (Sociological)** — The concept (from Ray Oldenburg) that people need a "third place" beyond home and work for community life. This is the foundational insight of the entire project. Status: validated as a real and growing need, especially post-pandemic.

2. **Community-Supported Commons vs. Commercial Business Tension** — Identified in Chat 2 as the "core mismatch": the project's mission (accessible, community-owned, fair wages, homeless services) pulls toward nonprofit/cooperative structure, while the funding model ($2M SBA loan) demands commercial returns. This tension was a recurring theme across all analysis. Status: unresolved, but Approach C addresses it directly.

3. **Bundled-Activity Value Proposition** — Offering diverse activities under one roof at a lower per-visit cost than single-purpose competitors. Competitive positioning: $5.50 average vs. $8-$15+ at specialized venues. Status: validated by direct price comparison research.

4. **Phased Rollout Strategy** — Not launching all 40+ activities simultaneously but staging them across months/years. Phases mentioned: sitting areas → board games → classes → events → expanded activities. Status: good strategic instinct, needs formal phasing plan.

5. **Community Co-Maintenance Model** — The vision that regulars would help teach classes, organize events, and maintain the space. Compared to: climbing gyms where members set routes, makerspaces where experienced users mentor. Status: viable model with precedent, but requires deliberate culture-building from day one.

6. **Tiered Pricing Architecture** — Children $2, Teens/College $5, Adults $8, Membership $25/month. Average expected payment: $5.50. Status: well-researched and competitive.

7. **Revenue Stream Diversification** — Entry fees, memberships, event tickets ($20-30), classes ($15), merchandise, facility rentals ($250-$1,200), theater/event space rental. Status: identified but not individually modeled for sensitivity.

8. **The Isolation Paradox** — Building community infrastructure requires community, but the lack of community is the problem. This paradox both validates the need and explains the execution challenge. Status: lived experience, not just theory.

### E. Methodology Concepts Applied

20. **Kill Steps** — Concrete, specific conditions under which an approach should be abandoned. Distinct from vague "if it doesn't work" — must be observable and testable. Applied to all three approaches. First introduced in Chat 1 analysis (via generic "kill-step questions"), formalized in current SRP deep-dive.

21. **Discriminating Tests** — Questions or experiments designed to tell WHICH approach is right, not just whether one approach is good. The key test: "Can you find collaborators?" — the answer to this effectively selects between approaches A, B, and C.

22. **Epistemic Status Labels** — Marking every claim as fact, inference, assumption, guess, or value judgment. Applied throughout the SRP analysis. Purpose: the person needs to see which parts of reasoning are load-bearing granite vs. scaffolding that could be swapped.

23. **Source Trust Levels** — Rating evidence sources as strong, moderate, weak, or flagged. Applied to Esri data (strong), primary foot traffic research (moderate-strong), cashflow template (moderate), insurance estimates (weak — inference, not quoted).

24. **Fragile Assumptions** — The specific assumptions most likely to break and with the highest impact if wrong. These become the first targets for the Reasoning phase. In this project: foot traffic conversion for a new venue, activity synergy, membership conversion rate, staffing cost sustainability, property availability.

25. **Genuine vs. Cosmetic Alternatives** — The test: "Would someone who believed Approach A find Approach B threatening?" If yes, genuinely distinct. If they'd shrug, it's cosmetic. Applied: A (full commercial) vs. C (cooperative) pass this test clearly.

26. **Phase Connectivity** — Ensuring Structure actually feeds Reasoning which actually feeds Presentation. The earlier conversations demonstrated the failure mode: Structure was skipped → Reasoning was disconnected from Weston's actual data → Presentation was generic advice disconnected from the real problem.

27. **Confidence Calibration** — Not just "I think this is likely" but a number tied to evidence quality. Applied: 80% market demand, 85% pricing, 95% solo failure, 65% full-scale viability at medium traffic, 35% cooperative attracting 100+ members, 70% sequenced recommendation.

28. **Adaptive Depth** — Choosing how much SRP structure to surface based on the problem's complexity. Light touch for simple tasks, visible structure for ambiguity, full SRP for complex multi-approach problems. This project warranted full SRP (3+ approaches, high stakes, multiple stakeholders/perspectives).

29. **Preserved Dissent** — When approaches genuinely disagree and evidence doesn't resolve it, the disagreement survives into presentation. Not collapsed into a false "best answer." Applied: "A is better if X, B is better if Y, C is better if Z."

## VII. METHODOLOGY NOTES FOR FUTURE SESSIONS

### What Worked Well in SRP Application
- Restating the goal before reasoning prevented the "answering the wrong question" problem from Chat 1-2
- Naming fragile assumptions upfront created natural test targets
- Kill-steps made each approach falsifiable rather than abstract
- CCC synthesis prevented forcing a false winner
- Confidence calibration tied to source trust made uncertainty visible rather than hidden

### What Needs Improvement
- The SRP skill references a methods library and combinations CSV that don't exist in the project — these should be created or the skill should be updated to work without them
- Sage-memory skill needs to be built as a companion to SRP — the SRP skill explicitly notes memory management as outside its scope
- Earlier conversations would have benefited from SRP discipline — the "jump to reasoning without structure" failure mode played out exactly as the skill predicts
- Source verification could be deeper — several "inference" claims (insurance costs, first-year traffic ramp) should be researched rather than estimated

### SAGE Facet Balance Observations
Looking back across all three conversations, the SAGE facets were unevenly applied:

- **Therapist** (agency/consent): Strong in current session (asked what Weston wanted before proceeding). Weak in earlier sessions (jumped to prescriptive advice).
- **Scientist** (competing hypotheses): Strong in SRP deep-dive. Absent in earlier sessions (single-hypothesis reasoning).
- **Generalist** (cross-domain patterns): Moderate — connected cooperative business models, makerspace precedents, community organizing patterns. Could draw more from other domains.
- **Engineer** (real-world feasibility): Strong throughout — consistently asked "how would this actually work?"
- **Philosopher** (assumptions/values): Strong in identifying the commons-vs-commercial tension. Could go deeper on what "community ownership" actually means in practice.
- **Artist/Teacher** (memorable framing): The "isolation paradox" is a good example. The sequenced B→A/C recommendation is clear. Room for more.
- **Comedian** (lightness): Barely present. These are serious topics, but a bit more warmth wouldn't hurt.
